# Why a Cooler City Needs More Than More Trees

Planting trees is one of the most visible ways a city can respond to hotter weather. Trees provide shade, cool the air, and make dense neighbourhoods more pleasant.

Yet a canopy target says little about whether people can escape the heat. New trees may be far from crowded footpaths, too young to shade a playground, or unable to stop nearby concrete from releasing heat after sunset. A city can meet its planting target while leaving the most difficult parts of the day unchanged.

Cooling plans should therefore respond to three features of daily experience: where people are exposed, how streets store heat, and when relief is needed.

## Shade the places people cannot avoid

A bus shelter protects passengers while they wait. It covers the queue rather than an arbitrary patch of pavement. It provides relief immediately while nearby trees mature.

The same principle changes where a city looks for planting opportunities. Instead of beginning with empty plots, planners can trace unavoidable journeys: the walk to school, the crossing outside a clinic, or the queue beside a food centre. Shade becomes useful when its location follows people's routines.

## Heat remains after the sun moves on

Dark paving absorbs sunlight throughout the afternoon. That stored energy warms nearby walls and walkways after sunset. The released heat then reaches homes just as residents return for the evening.

Trees alone cannot interrupt every step in that chain. Lighter roofs can reflect incoming energy, planted ground can replace exposed paving, and permeable surfaces can retain water for evaporation. These changes matter because evening conditions depend partly on what the neighbourhood absorbed earlier.

## Averages hide the difficult hours

Heat exposure changes across the day:

- Morning: children walk to school as streets begin to warm.
- Afternoon: outdoor workers face strong sun with little choice about when to stop.
- Evening: commuters return through surfaces that have stored heat for hours.
- Night: some homes remain uncomfortable even after outdoor temperatures fall.

A single afternoon reading cannot represent all four periods. Sensors can record when a courtyard peaks, resident reports can reveal which bedrooms stay warm, and observations can show when shade disappears from a busy queue. Together, this evidence shows when an intervention must work, not merely where the map looks hottest.

## Follow one journey

Imagine a resident leaving home at six in the evening. Her route begins in a block that retained heat through the afternoon. She crosses an exposed junction, waits beside a dark road, and finally reaches a shaded park.

A canopy map might describe the neighbourhood as well served because the park contains mature trees. Her journey tells a different story. Most of her exposure occurs before she reaches them.

The route could be improved in several ways: reflective material on the block's roof, shade across the junction, and a shelter positioned over the bus queue. Evening measurements could then test whether those changes help at the time she travels.

No single intervention explains the result. The journey reveals where shade, cooler surfaces, and better timing must meet.

## Design for the temperature people experience

Tree planting remains valuable, but residents do not experience heat as a canopy total. They experience it on particular journeys, beside particular surfaces, at particular times.

A useful cooling plan follows that experience. Its success is measured by whether people can move, wait, work, and rest more safely through a hotter day.
