# Sentence review

Checks how each sentence carries its meaning: whether characters sit in subjects and actions in verbs, whether the main verb arrives early, whether sentences end on their most important information, whether key terms keep one form, whether words are plain and precise, and whether every sentence has one reading.

**When to use:** Run after the writing patterns review, once the order of ideas is settled. The architecture review's announced and unannounced terms supply the key-term list. Topic-Comment handoffs between sentences belong to the patterns review. This review judges each sentence on its own and tracks key terms across the essay.

**Score categories:** `characters_and_actions`, `stress_position`, `key_term_consistency`, `word_choice`, `syntactic_clarity`

## What to look for

### 1. Characters and actions
Each sentence tells a story about someone doing something. Its main character should be the grammatical subject, and its key action should be the main verb.
- Keep the subject short. Reach the main verb within the first eight words. A short opener that links back to the previous sentence or states a condition may come before the subject.
- Characters hide in possessives ("the writer's fear"), prepositional phrases ("the recommendation from his publisher"), and adjectives ("congressional support"). Move them into the subject.
- Actions hide in nouns ending in -tion, -ment, -ence, -ance, -ity, or -ness, which then sit next to empty verbs: is, was made, took place, occurred, resulted in, led to, there is. Turn the action back into the verb.
- A nominalization is fine when it does one of four jobs: it sums up earlier sentences ("These theories"), it replaces "the fact that", it is the object of a verb, or it names a concept so familiar that it acts as a character. A nominalization that performs the verb's action as a subject is not one of these.
- Assume active voice. Passive is fine when the actor is unknown or doesn't matter, or when it keeps familiar information first and moves new information to the end.

### 2. Stress position
The end of a sentence gets emphasis. Each sentence should begin with short, familiar material and end on its newest, most important, or most complex information.
- Flag sentences that spend the final position on throwaway material: prepositions, hedges ("in most cases"), signposts ("as noted above"), trailing time or source phrases, or a low-value agent ("by me").
- Flag sentences that put a long, unfamiliar subject first when the order could be reversed.
- Judge order inside the sentence only. Whether the ending is picked up by the next sentence is the patterns review's job.

### 3. Key-term consistency
Once a key term is introduced, refer to it only by forms readers have already learned. A term may have up to two or three variant phrases (for example "stereotrodes" and "these electrodes"), but every variant must be introduced early, next to the term, so readers learn they mean the same thing. Morphological variants of the same word are always fine (community and communal, expose and exposure). A synonym that first appears later (community, then later "groups") is drift, because readers take a new word to mean a new concept.
- Key terms are the concepts an Index announces, terms the essay defines, and the names of things the argument tracks. Ordinary references to people and objects may use pronouns or shortened names if each has one clear referent.
- Don't use one word with two meanings in the argument.
- Define an unfamiliar term or abbreviation at first use. After that, don't switch between the full and short forms.

### 4. Word choice
Choose the plain, precise, concrete word the intended reader would use.
- Plain words: use "use", not "utilize" or "leverage", and "start", not "commence". A longer word is fine only when it carries a distinct meaning.
- Concision: one word replaces a phrase ("to", not "in order to"; "describes", not "provides a description of"). Cut fillers such as "it is important to note that".
- Precision: name the specific thing, actor, or quantity instead of a vague or overloaded noun (solution, support, aspect, issue). Cut subjective intensifiers such as simply, very, clearly, and obviously.
- One reading: avoid words that read two ways. Use "because", not "since" or "as"; "although", not "while", for contrast; and "after", not "once".
- Global readers: avoid idioms, colloquialisms, culture-bound references, and idiomatic phrasal verbs where a single verb exists ("use", not "make use of"). Standard phrasal verbs such as "set up" are fine. Define jargon or write around it.
- Value words and connectives are instructions. "Dogmas" and "traditions" can name the same thing but tell readers opposite things about it. "However" signals a problem, while "furthermore" signals more of the same. Flag one that sends the wrong instruction.
- A metaphor is fine when it carries the argument and the essay makes the comparison explicit. Flag decorative or stock figures of speech.

### 5. Syntactic clarity
Every sentence should have one reading on the first pass.
- One idea per sentence. A long sentence is fine when its main clause comes first and the rest develops that idea. Split a sentence whose clauses branch into a second idea.
- Put a modifier next to what it modifies, especially "only", "not", prepositional phrases, and relative clauses.
- Keep noun strings to three words. Unpack longer ones with "of" or a relative clause.
- Make every pronoun point to one noun. Don't use "this" or "which" to refer to a whole clause. Repeat the noun when in doubt.
- Keep the small words that show structure: "that", articles, "then", and a repeated article or preposition in a series. Don't drop verbs or subjects.
- Make clear which parts "and" or "or" joins.
- Don't let a parenthetical or dash interruption separate a subject from its verb. Text inside dashes or parentheses should be removable without breaking the sentence.
- Revise dangling or ambiguous -ing and -ed phrases.
- Put a condition before the claim or instruction it governs. Prefer positive statements to stacked negatives.

## How to review

1. Take the key-term list from the architecture review's announced and unannounced terms. If that review hasn't run, build the list from the introduction's Point sentence, the section Indexes, and the terms the essay defines.
2. Review every sentence of the prose, including the introduction and conclusion. Skip headings, list labels, quotations from other authors, and code.
3. For each sentence, find the main subject and main verb and count the words before the main verb.
4. Trace each key term from its first form to every later form. Record morphological variants and early-introduced variants as consistent, and any later-introduced synonym as drift.
5. Record a finding only where a rule applies. Quote the whole sentence exactly, with the source capitalization and punctuation. Never insert ellipses, brackets, or paraphrases into a quote.
6. Rate each finding minor or major. A minor finding still reads correctly on the first pass but costs effort. A major finding forces a reread, invites a misreading, or loses the character, action, or key term.
7. Assign each problem to the one category that best explains it. For example, an abstract subject that delays the verb belongs under characters and actions, not syntactic clarity.
8. Don't flag a rare, purposeful departure that serves the reader, such as a delayed main clause that builds suspense, a cumulative list, or repetition for parallelism. Name it in a finding note instead.
9. Recommend a rewrite only if it reads naturally to a fluent reader and keeps the meaning. A rewrite that satisfies a rule but sounds stilted is not an improvement. Fix the sentence another way or leave it alone.
10. Return one score per category with the evidence that supports it.

## Scoring
Score each category independently by choosing the description that best matches the evidence. Base scores on how consistently the sentences follow the framework, not on general readability. Don't count length, contractions, or punctuation choices as faults in themselves.

### Characters and actions
- 5: sentences consistently name their main character in a short subject and carry the key action in a main verb that arrives early; every nominalization and passive does identifiable work.
- 4: isolated sentences hide a character or action or delay the verb, without slowing their passage.
- 3: recurring hidden characters, empty verbs, or late verbs make several passages abstract, though actors and actions can still be recovered.
- 2: many sentences open with long abstract subjects and empty verbs; readers must reconstruct who does what.
- 1: actors and actions are routinely missing from subjects and verbs.

### Stress position
- 5: sentences consistently begin with short, familiar material and end on their newest or most important information.
- 4: isolated sentences end on throwaway words or open with new material.
- 3: recurring weak endings or front-loaded subjects blunt emphasis in several passages.
- 2: emphasis routinely falls on hedges, function words, or old information; key points sit mid-sentence.
- 1: sentence order routinely works against the content.

### Key-term consistency
- 5: every key term keeps the forms introduced with it (at most two or three early variants, plus morphological variants); each word keeps one meaning; unfamiliar terms are defined at first use.
- 4: one key term drifts once, or one term goes undefined, without raising doubt about the concept.
- 3: several synonyms or alternating forms make readers wonder whether new concepts have appeared.
- 2: central key terms change name repeatedly, or one word carries two meanings in the argument.
- 1: the essay has no stable vocabulary for its key concepts.

### Word choice
- 5: words are plain, precise, and concrete for the intended reader; idioms, filler, and ambiguous words are absent or deliberate; value words and connectives send the intended instruction.
- 4: isolated inflated, vague, or idiomatic words, none of which cause a misreading.
- 3: recurring inflated or vague words, filler, or idioms make several passages harder to read or translate.
- 2: abstraction, jargon, or idiom dominates, and the precise meaning is often unclear.
- 1: word choice routinely obscures meaning.

### Syntactic clarity
- 5: every sentence reads one way on the first pass; modifiers, pronouns, and conjunctions attach clearly, and structure words are present.
- 4: isolated ambiguities or overloaded sentences that context quickly resolves.
- 3: recurring ambiguous references, misplaced modifiers, or multi-idea sentences force rereading in several passages.
- 2: many sentences have two readings or bundle unrelated ideas.
- 1: sentence structure routinely prevents a single reading.

## Worked examples

### 1. Characters and actions
Good: "Dark paving absorbs sunlight throughout the afternoon." The character is the subject, the action is the verb, and the verb is the third word.
Good passive: "Its success is measured by whether people can move, wait, work, and rest more safely through a hotter day." The known plan stays in the subject, and the new list closes the sentence.
Bad: "The absorption of sunlight by dark paving takes place throughout the afternoon." The action hides in "absorption", and the empty verb "takes place" arrives eighth.

### 2. Stress position
Good: "A city can meet its planting target while leaving the most difficult parts of the day unchanged." The sentence ends on the cost the paragraph builds toward.
Bad: "A city can leave the most difficult parts of the day unchanged while meeting its planting target, in many cases." The cost sits mid-sentence, and a hedge takes the final position.

### 3. Key-term consistency
Good: "where people are exposed" continues as "Heat exposure" and "Most of her exposure". These are morphological variants of one term.
Bad: rewrite the last of these as "Most of her discomfort occurs before she reaches them." Readers must decide whether discomfort is a new concept.

### 4. Word choice
Good: "A bus shelter protects passengers while they wait." "While" refers to time here, so it has one reading.
Bad: "A bus shelter can be leveraged to move the needle on passenger thermal comfort." An inflated verb, an idiom, and an abstraction replace a plain action.

### 5. Syntactic clarity
Good: "Sensors can record when a courtyard peaks, resident reports can reveal which bedrooms stay warm, and observations can show when shade disappears from a busy queue." The sentence is long, but each clause has its own subject and verb and develops one idea.
Bad: "Planners only need sensor data. This explains when relief is needed." "Only" attaches to the wrong word, and "This" points at a whole sentence.

The examples refer to the worked example essay in [references/example-essay.md](references/example-essay.md); read it when calibrating a review.
